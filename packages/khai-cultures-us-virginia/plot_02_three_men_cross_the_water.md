## Cue

Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James Townsend take a small boat into Hampton Roads at night. They have been forced to build Confederate defenses. Across the water stands Fort Monroe, still held by the United States. They row toward it before either army has decided what freedom will mean.

## Action

On May 23, 1861, the three men escaped slavery and reached [Fort Monroe](place_fort_monroe.md). The next day the man claiming them arrived under a flag of truce and demanded their return under the Fugitive Slave Act. Major General Benjamin Butler refused. Because Virginia claimed to have left the United States and was using enslaved labor for war, he classified the men as contraband of war.

Butler's formula gave the army an administrative reason not to send them back. Baker, Mallory, and Townsend had already supplied the decisive act. They assessed two armed shores, crossed the water, presented themselves at the fort, and forced a commander to answer what their freedom required. The policy followed the deed.

Word spread. Hundreds and then thousands of freedom seekers came to Fort Monroe, and camps grew around it. The improvised category of contraband was not equality, citizenship, or the end of exploitation. It was nevertheless a breach opened by people refusing to remain property, one that pushed wartime policy toward confiscation and emancipation.

## Tension

The usual record names Butler's decision. The originating action belongs to Baker, Mallory, and Townsend. Virginia's law had defined them as property; the Confederacy used their labor as military material; the fort first received them as contraband. Their crossing made every category trail behind the fact that they had taken possession of themselves.
