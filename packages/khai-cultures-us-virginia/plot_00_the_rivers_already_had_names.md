# Plot: the rivers already had names

## Cue

Spring shad enter the rivers from the Chesapeake. Nets wait in the tidal water, corn is planted on the cleared ground above it, and clay taken from that same ground is coiled into a pot. The river is road, pantry, boundary, and relation. It already has a name.

## Action

For thousands of years before an English settlement called the country Virginia, Algonquian-speaking peoples lived along the Powhatan, Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Chickahominy, Rappahannock, and Potomac river systems. They fished, hunted, farmed corn, beans, and squash, made pottery, and joined towns through kinship, exchange, and political obligation. The waterways were not empty approaches to a colony. They were the inhabited structure of Tsenacommacah, the densely connected country encountered by the English in 1607.

The people did not disappear when their country was renamed. The Pamunkey reservation stands on the river that bears the nation's name, on land continuously held through colonial and federal regimes. Pamunkey potters kept making with local clay, sometimes under pressure to satisfy an outside market's idea of what Native work should look like, and later built a school and guild to carry the practice forward. Fishing, pottery, government, family, and language work remain acts in the present, not exhibits from a completed past.

This is the origin beneath every later Virginia plot. Tobacco could become an export because settlers entered an already cultivated river world. Three men in plot 2 could cross to Fort Monroe because water connected shore to shore. Richmond, Hampton Roads, and the data corridor all inherit a geography that Indigenous communities knew as lived country before anyone called it dominion.

## Tension

Virginia often begins its own story when English ships arrive. [Lena](persona_lena.md) lives inside the longer record: a present-day maker and river worker whose nation survived the maps, markets, and official histories that repeatedly placed it in the past tense. [The Chesapeake Bay plan](plan_chesapeake_bay_cleanup.md) can treat the watershed as a technical object, but the first obligation is older and more difficult - to recognize whose lived relation to the water has already lasted longest.
