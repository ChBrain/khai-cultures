# Plot: the microphone on State Street

## Cue

Musicians answer a newspaper advertisement and carry guitars, fiddles, banjos, autoharps, and songs into a hat warehouse on [State Street](place_bristol_state_street.md). A microphone waits upstairs. The state line runs down the street below, but the music has crossed it for years.

## Action

In the summer of 1927, Victor Talking Machine Company producer Ralph Peer set up portable electrical recording equipment in Bristol. Performers came from Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, and Kentucky. The sessions gave the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers their first commercial recordings and sent music made in homes, churches, fields, dances, medicine shows, and local broadcasts into a national market.

The microphone did not invent a pure white mountain tradition. The banjo carried African origins; Black and white musicians exchanged repertoire, technique, labor, and performance space across Appalachia even when segregation separated their names and pay. Commercial country music narrowed that braided inheritance into market categories, making some artists legible as founders while leaving many sources unnamed.

[A Bristol session disc](piece_bristol_session_disc.md) could travel much farther than the singer who made it. Pressing and radio turned a local performance into a repeatable object, while [the string-band session](process_string_band_session.md) still depended on bodies listening and adjusting in one room. Southwest Virginia entered the national ear through that exchange between intimate practice and industrial reproduction.

## Stage

- [State Street](place_bristol_state_street.md): a border used as a meeting place, with Virginia on one side and Tennessee on the other.
- [the string-band session](process_string_band_session.md): repertoire, timing, and instrumental roles negotiated by ear before the microphone fixes one take.
- [a Bristol session disc](piece_bristol_session_disc.md): a local performance made portable by recording and manufacture.
- [mountain exchange](position_mountain_exchange.md): belonging made through borrowed tunes and answered phrases rather than cultural purity.

## Tension

The Bristol Sessions are called a birth because the records made a market visible. The music had many parents before the machines arrived, including Black musicians whose work the market often filed somewhere else. [Caleb](persona_caleb.md) works in the medium that followed the disc, making radio from the same border city. His obligation is not to protect an origin myth. It is to keep the exchange audible, including the hands commercial history left off the label.
