# Plot: Stono 1739

## Cue

Early on Sunday, 9 September 1739, about twenty men gather by the bridge over the Stono River, twenty miles from Charles Town. The man the colonists will call Jemmy, or Cato, leads them to a store that sells guns and powder. They take both, and turn south.

## Action

They march along the road toward Spanish Florida, where the governor at St. Augustine has promised freedom to anyone who escapes the English colonies, with a banner up and two drums beating, calling out Liberty. Men come off the plantations they pass and join them, until there are perhaps sixty or a hundred. Historians who have read the accounts against what is known of Kongo think many were Kongolese, some of them soldiers and some Catholic, and that the day before had been the feast of the Virgin's birth.

By the afternoon the militia catches them in a field near the Edisto River. Most are killed there or hunted down in the weeks after, and heads are set on mileposts along the road. Some twenty colonists are dead, and dozens of the insurgents.

The assembly answers in May 1740 with the Negro Act, a rewriting of [the slave code](piece_the_slave_code.md). It forbids the enslaved to gather, to raise their own food, to earn money and to learn to write, and it forbids drums, because drums had carried the march. It stands, in its essentials, until 1865. And the colony puts a duty on new Africans for a few years, because a majority that had shown it could march was now a thing to be counted.

## Stage

- [plantation resistance](position_plantation_resistance.md): the refusal that runs under this play from its first decades, here as a march with drums.
- [the slave code](piece_the_slave_code.md): rewritten in 1740 to silence the instruments the march had used.

## Tension

The largest rising of enslaved people in the British mainland colonies lasted a single day and is remembered mostly through what it provoked. The law that followed it is better documented than the men who caused it, whose own words are in no record at all. The colony learned from Stono that its majority could act; what it chose to do with that knowledge was to forbid them the drum and the pen, which is to say it made sure the next account would also be written by somebody else.
