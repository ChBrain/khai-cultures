# Plot: The School Bus 1947

## Taxonomy

[play_south_carolina](play_south_carolina.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

In 1947 Levi Pearson, a farmer near Summerton in Clarendon County, asks the county for a school bus. White children in the county ride to school. Black children walk, some of them nine miles each way, and the year before their parents had pooled seven hundred dollars to buy a used bus of their own, which keeps breaking down.

## Action

The request goes nowhere, and Pearson goes to court with the help of the Reverend J. A. DeLaine, a teacher and minister in the county, and the NAACP. The case is thrown out because Pearson's land sits on a district line and he pays his taxes on the wrong side of it. Nobody in the county will lend him money or buy his timber afterwards.

DeLaine goes back to the parents and asks for more, not a bus but equal schools, and twenty of them sign. Harry Briggs, a Navy veteran who pumps gas, is first on the list by the alphabet, and the case carries his name. He loses his job; so does his wife Eliza, and so do others who signed. DeLaine is fired from his school, his house is burned, and in 1955, after shots are fired at his home in Lake City and he fires back, he leaves the state and is charged with assault. South Carolina does not drop the charge until 2000.

Briggs v. Elliott loses in 1951 before three federal judges, with one of them, J. Waties Waring of Charleston, writing in dissent that segregation is per se inequality. It goes to the Supreme Court as the first of the five cases decided together as Brown v. Board of Education in 1954.

## Tension

The case that ended legal segregation in American schools began with a farmer asking for a bus, and the people who asked lost their livelihoods and in one case had to flee. Brown carries the name of a family in Kansas; the first of its cases came from a county in South Carolina where the parents had already bought a broken bus themselves. Seventy years later the public schools of Summerton are attended almost entirely by Black children, and many white families send theirs to a private academy founded in the 1960s. The bus came. The schools it was meant to reach were left to the people who had asked for it.
