# Plot: the wharf

## Taxonomy

[play_south_carolina](play_south_carolina.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

On 27 June 2023 the International African American Museum opens in Charleston on the site of Gadsden's Wharf, and the building does not touch the ground. It stands thirteen feet up on eighteen pillars, because its architect decided that the ground under it would always matter more than anything built on top.

## Action

The wharf was built by Christopher Gadsden, a merchant and a patriot of the Revolution who designed the yellow flag with the rattlesnake and the words Don't Tread on Me. In 1806 the city ordered that every slave ship landing in Charleston use his wharf, and in the two years before the federal ban of 1808 tens of thousands of Africans were brought ashore there, hundreds of whom died in the warehouses in the cold of one winter. Beneath the museum a shallow pool holds the outlines of bodies laid out as in the diagram of the slave ship Brookes. Water fills it and drains away, covering the figures and showing them again.

A few streets away [the Charleston Historic District](place_charleston_historic_district.md) sells the same city as charm: the first historic zoning in the country, written in 1931 to keep the old houses as they were, and kept ever since by [historic masonry](process_historic_masonry.md) and people like [Charlotte](persona_charlotte.md), who repoints the brick with the soft lime mortar the houses were built with. The city council apologised for Charleston's part in slavery in 2018, by seven votes to five. On the highway north of the harbour [Elijah](persona_elijah.md) sells sweetgrass baskets at a roadside stand, on land his family has held since emancipation and is taxed on as if it were a resort.

And the water is coming in. The low streets of the peninsula flood on high tides with no rain at all, many more days a year than they did a generation ago, and [the salt marsh plan](plan_lowcountry_salt_marsh_conservation.md) is the state's bet that a living shoreline can hold what a seawall cannot.

## Stage

- [the Charleston Historic District](place_charleston_historic_district.md): the preserved city that sold its streets as charm for a century, with the wharf at its edge.
- [historic stewardship](position_historic_stewardship.md): the habit of keeping what was built, now asked to keep where people were landed as well.
- [historic masonry](process_historic_masonry.md): the slow work of soft mortar and old brick that keeps the district standing.
- [Charlotte](persona_charlotte.md): the preservation architect whose craft keeps the houses and whose city has only lately counted the wharf.
- [Elijah](persona_elijah.md): the basket maker on the highway, whose land is worth more to everyone else than to the people who have it.
- [the salt marsh plan](plan_lowcountry_salt_marsh_conservation.md): the wager that marsh grass will hold the coast when the tides keep rising.

## Tension

Charleston preserved itself before any other American city did and became beautiful at it, and for most of that century what it preserved was the houses and not the wharf. The museum is the city saying the other half aloud, on the spot where the half was landed, in a building that will not stand on it. Whether that is the beginning of reading the whole record or a second preserved site beside the first, admired by the same visitors, is not settled. Nor is whether the low city, wharf and district alike, will still be above the tide when it is.
