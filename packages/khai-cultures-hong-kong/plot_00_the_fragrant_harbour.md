# Plot: The Fragrant Harbour

## Cue

Long before any treaty, a family is born, married and buried on a boat. They work the water of the estuary where the Pearl River meets the sea, they are taxed as a people apart, and they are not permitted to hold land. Nothing they own is fixed to the ground. Everything they have floats, and moves when the weather does.

## Action

This coast was worked from the water for centuries before it was worked from the shore. Salt was raked from the shallows and sold inland. Pearls were dived under the Southern Han. And out of a small anchorage on the west of the island went the cargo that gave the place its name: **incense wood**, cut in the hills of the delta and shipped from here to Canton and beyond. Heung Gong. **The fragrant harbour.**

That is what the name records. Not a founder, not a dynasty, not a battle - **a cargo.** The place is named for something that passed through it on its way to somewhere else.

And the water is the whole of it. At the mouth of the Pearl River, which is the outlet of Canton and therefore of the trade of a continent, lies one of the finest natural anchorages on the coast of China: deep, sheltered on both flanks, and never frozen. The Qing court looked at the island and called it a barren rock, because what it counted was land that could be farmed. The British looked at the same island and counted the water beside it.

**Neither of them made the harbour.** It was already there, it was already carrying other people's goods, and it had already given the place the name it still has.

## Tension

A place named for a cargo is a place defined by what crosses it, and that is the whole inheritance. The entrepot instinct was not brought here by an empire; the empire recognised it and put a flag on it. From that comes everything this city is good at: reading a trade, moving early, holding nothing so tightly that it cannot be put on a boat.

And from that comes the shadow, which is older than any of its governments. **What is valued for its throughput is valued by other people for what it does for them.** A port is always somebody's asset - the court's, the company's, the crown's, the country's - and the question of what the people living on it might want for themselves is the last question anyone asks, if it is asked at all. Being useful is what has kept this place alive at every turn. It is also the reason its own wishes have never once been the point.
