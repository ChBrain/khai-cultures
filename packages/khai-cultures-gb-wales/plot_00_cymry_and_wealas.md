# Plot: Cymry and Wealas, from 550

## Cue

The Britons of the island, who had been there before Rome and were still there after it, find newcomers holding the east and themselves holding the west, and they begin calling one another by a word that means the people who belong together, at the same time as the newcomers begin calling them a word that means the strangers.

## Action

Both names stick, and they contradict each other for fifteen hundred years. _Cymry_, from a British word for fellow-countrymen, is what this people call themselves; _wealas_, the English word for the foreigner and the Roman-speaker, is where Wales comes from, so the country is known to the world by a name meaning _not us_ and to itself by a name meaning _each other_. In the same century the tongue finishes separating out of British and becomes Welsh, and men start composing in it. What survives from them is the oldest poetry in any living tongue of these islands, and the greatest of it, Aneirin's, is an elegy for a war-band out of Din Eidyn cut down at Catraeth. The first thing Welsh literature does is mourn a country in what is now somebody else's.

## Tension

Wales begins with a people named twice and a literature that opens in mourning, and both are still load bearing. _Hiraeth_ is not a mood this culture acquired from its defeats; it is in the earliest verse it has, written when the loss being mourned was the Old North and not yet anything nearer. And a nation that has always known what its neighbours call it has never had the option of forgetting that there are two accounts of who it is.
