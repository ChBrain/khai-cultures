# Plot: The Laws Written in Taverns, 1863

## Cue

The schools and the clubs have each been playing their own version of the same rough game for a generation, and nobody can get a fixture against a stranger without an argument first, so on an evening in October the secretaries of eleven London clubs take a room at the Freemasons' Tavern in Great Queen Street to agree one set of rules.

## Action

They argue over six meetings, and the argument is about whether a man may run with the ball in his hands and whether he may kick another man's shins. The clubs that want both walk out and go on to make a second code of their own; the rest write the Laws of the Game and put a Football Association behind them. It is the same instinct that had already made the Marylebone Cricket Club the keeper of cricket's Laws, and that keeps _Gentlemen v Players_ on the fixture list from 1806 to 1962, amateur against professional, the two sides for many years using separate dressing rooms and walking onto the field through separate gates. The country codifies its games before it codifies much else, exports the codes to the world, and gets in return the highest praise and the sharpest insult in its language for conduct having nothing to do with sport: _it simply isn't cricket._

## Tension

Writing the rules down settles the fixture and unsettles everything else. A code that says how the game is played says who may play it, and the amateur ideal that looks like disinterest from the inside looks from the outside like a rule that only men with private incomes can afford to keep. England gives the world the best argument it has for playing fairly and, in the same breath, one of the neatest instruments of class it has ever built, and it has never fully separated the two.
