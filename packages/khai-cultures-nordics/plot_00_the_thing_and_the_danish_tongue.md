# Plot: The Thing and the Danish Tongue, 930 to 1300

## Taxonomy

[play_nordics](play_nordics.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Free men ride to a plain in the summer, camp there for a fortnight, and settle their disputes and make their law in the open air by argument in front of everybody, and the man whose job it is to know the law has no army and no office, only the requirement to recite a third of it aloud each year from memory.

## Action

They do this everywhere in the north and they do it in one language. Iceland's assembly at Þingvellir from around 930 is the version that kept its records and its site, but the thing is not Icelandic: there are assemblies across Norway, at Uppsala and in the Swedish shires, and in the Danish lands, and the same word covers all of them. The speech they are conducted in is one speech, and its own speakers call it _dǫnsk tunga_, the Danish tongue, in Iceland and Norway as readily as in Denmark, which tells you they thought of it as one thing with a borrowed name rather than as three. It is not that these were free societies in any modern sense; they had slaves and feuds and no notion of equality. It is that the deciding was done by assembly in a shared language, in public, by men who then went home, and that arrangement was normal here and exotic almost everywhere else.

## Stage

- [Iceland](@chbrain/khai-cultures-iceland/play_iceland.md): the assembly that survived, wrote itself down, and is still standing on the ground it met on.
- [Denmark](@chbrain/khai-cultures-denmark/play_denmark.md): the country the shared language was named after by people who were not in it.
- [det danske sprog](@chbrain/khai-cultures-tongues/da/position_language_da.md): the modern descendant of the one that got its name onto the whole family.
- [Norway](@chbrain/khai-cultures-norway/play_norway.md): the middle of the speech area, and the route the settlement of Iceland came out of.

## Tension

Everything the world now calls Nordic is credited to the twentieth century and to the welfare state, and the two habits underneath it are eight hundred years older: settling things by assembly rather than by command, and doing it in a language your neighbour can follow without study. Neither of those survived intact. The assemblies were absorbed into kingdoms, the one language became four, and Finland was never in either of them, which is the fact the rest of this play has to keep accounting for.
