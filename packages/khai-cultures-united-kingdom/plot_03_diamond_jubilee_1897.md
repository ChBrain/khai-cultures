# Plot: the Diamond Jubilee, 1897

## Taxonomy

[play_united_kingdom](play_united_kingdom.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Sixty years into the reign, the empire decides to look at itself. The Colonial Secretary turns the Queen's jubilee into an imperial one and summons the premiers of the self-governing colonies and troops from every possession, so that on the twenty-second of June 1897 the procession through [London](place_london.md) is a parade of the quarter of the world's people ruled from this island.

## Action

Fifty thousand troops march, from Canadian hussars to Hausa riflemen, past crowds who have never seen the places they come from and will never need to. The empire is not a policy that day; it is a feeling, and the feeling is that this is simply how the world is arranged. What it has already done to the country at home is quieter and more permanent. The tea drunk in every kitchen is grown in Assam and Ceylon on land taken for the purpose and sweetened with Caribbean sugar, so the most domestic thing in Britain is an imported one. The language has gone out on the same ships and come back with bungalow, pyjamas, loot and shampoo in it. And in the same summer, commissioned for the jubilee and published in The Times a week after the procession, Kipling writes Recessional, which is not a hymn to any of it but a warning that it will pass - the empire's own laureate, at its high-water mark, telling it that Nineveh and Tyre are the precedent.

## Stage

- [London](place_london.md): the capital arranging the parade, and the one place from which the whole of it could be seen at once.
- [a cup of tea](process_tea.md): the empire on a kitchen table, grown abroad on land taken for it and drunk at home as the most English thing there is.
- [William Shakespeare](persona_shakespeare.md): the tongue that went out on the ships, carried as the possession the empire was proudest of and the one thing it left everywhere that outlasted it.

## Tension

The play's Arc says the island became a global power and its Stakes speak of the world it once ruled, and until now no scene here has admitted the empire existed. It is staged at the moment of most self-regard on purpose, because that is when what it cost is least visible from London and most visible everywhere else: a procession is not a famine, a massacre or a partition, and all three are in the same decades and not in the parade. What the jubilee shows honestly is the mood - that this arrangement felt permanent to the people in the crowd - and Recessional shows that even in the mood there was somebody saying it would not be.
