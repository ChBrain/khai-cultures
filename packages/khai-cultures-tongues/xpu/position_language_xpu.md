# Position: 𐤊𐤍𐤏𐤍𐤌

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

A Canaanite grammar, which is to say a close relative of Hebrew and the direct
continuation of Phoenician in the west. Punic is what the Phoenician of Tyre
became after Carthage outgrew the city that founded it: the same triliteral
roots, the same prefix and suffix conjugations, the same construct chain that
binds two nouns into a possession without a preposition.

It has the name its speakers used, which is not the name history gave it. Rome
called them Poeni and the language Punic after them. Augustine, four centuries
after Carthage fell, wrote that if you asked the country people around Hippo
what they were, they answered in their own language: Chanani - Canaanites. The
declared name here is theirs, in their own letters, and the familiar one is the
enemy's.

It has a body of text far larger than its reputation: thousands of inscriptions
from Carthage, the North African coast, Sardinia, Sicily and Malta, the
bilingual stones that made the script readable at all, and one passage of
running speech preserved by accident in a Roman comedy, where Plautus gave a
Carthaginian character lines in his own tongue and Latin copyists carried them
for two thousand years without understanding a word.

## Orders

It requires the reader to supply the vowels. The Phoenician abjad writes
consonants and nothing else, and Punic was written in it for its whole life. Neo-
Punic, the later cursive, began pressing some consonant letters into service as
vowel markers, but never systematically. What the stones give is a skeleton.

It also requires the reader to come to it through other people's alphabets. The
vowels that are known are known because Greeks and Romans wrote Punic names and
words down in scripts that did record them, and because Plautus's scribes copied
sounds they could not parse. The language is reconstructed inward, from the
transcriptions of foreigners.

## Loses

It lost its speakers, and the loss took longer than the histories imply.
Carthage was destroyed in 146 BC and the language went on being spoken in the
North African countryside for something like five hundred years after, under
Roman rule, in the villages rather than the cities, until it thinned out
somewhere around the fifth century of the common era.

What it lost with them is the sound. No native speaker has said a Punic sentence
in fifteen hundred years, and no transcription is dense enough to give the
prosody back. It also lost most of what was written on anything but stone: a
mercantile civilisation kept its accounts on materials that do not survive a
North African climate, so what remains is disproportionately the formal register

- dedications, votives, epitaphs, the words people chose for permanence.

## Drives

It drives whoever approaches it to read rather than to listen, and to accept a
text that is certain in its consonants and arguable in everything else. That is
not a defect of the record, it is the condition of the record, and a reader who
will not work under it cannot work here at all.

And it drives a house like this one to be exact about what death means for a
tongue. Punic is dead in the mouth and open on the page. Those are different
facts, and only one of them is usually said.
