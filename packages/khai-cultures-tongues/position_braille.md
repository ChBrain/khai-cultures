# Position: braille

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

Six dots in two columns, sixty-three combinations and a blank, in a cell small
enough that a fingertip covers it whole. That last constraint is the design and
everything else follows from it: the cell is the size of the sensing organ, so a
reader takes in exactly one character at a time and never any part of the next.

No language of its own, and no allegiance to one. The same cells carry English,
French, Arabic, Chinese, Greek and Hebrew; they carry mathematics in their own
codes and music in a notation a Japanese and a Brazilian player read the same
way. The first ten letters are the same cells in nearly every alphabetic braille
in the world, because they follow the assignment a nineteenth-century French
teenager made and nobody has found a reason to renumber. What sits on top of that
base is per language and per subject, and the base does not care which.

Contractions, which are what make it usable rather than merely possible. A single
cell can carry a whole common word, a two-cell sequence a longer one, and the
frequent letter groups have signs of their own. Without them the medium is too
slow to read at speed and too bulky to shelve; with them a fluent reader keeps up
with speech and pays for it by learning a second layer of spelling on top of
whatever the language already has.

And an identity between reading and writing that print cannot claim of
handwriting: the dots the finger reads are the dots the hand or the machine
punches, in the same arrangement, so whoever can read this can write it.

## Orders

It orders one character at a time, forward. There is no glance, no peripheral
vision, no taking in the shape of a page before starting on it. A reader cannot
skim, cannot look ahead to see how long a sentence is, and cannot flick back to a
half-remembered line without hunting for it by hand. Print lets a reader cheat
constantly and quietly; this does not allow it at all.

It therefore orders memory. Because the start of a sentence is behind the finger
rather than still on the page, a reader has to hold what has been read rather
than rely on looking again, and holds a great deal more of it than a sighted
reader of the same text does.

And it orders bulk. The cell cannot shrink below what a fingertip resolves, so a
printed book becomes several volumes, and every practical question, what gets
transcribed, what a library stocks, what a child carries to school, is settled by
a physical constant no technology has been able to argue with.

## Loses

Three of the five channels, and it never had them. A tongue is spoken and heard,
written and read, and thought in. This one is written and read and that is all:
nobody speaks braille, nobody hears it, and nobody thinks in it, because the
thinking underneath belongs to whatever language is in the cells. It is a tongue
without being a language, which is a thing the other eighty-eight positions in
this package are not, and it means the depth its holder stands at can only ever
be measured in two of the five.

It loses the glance, and with it the page. Layout, typography, a heading seen
before it is read, a table taken in as a shape, a footnote noticed without being
sought: that is print doing two-dimensional work, and this is strictly
one-dimensional in time. What print carries in the arrangement of the ink, this
must carry in words or lose.

And it loses ground to speech. Audio arrives faster, costs nothing to make and
needs no transcription, and the share of blind children taught to read this has
fallen a long way from where it was. What goes with it is not access to books,
which audio supplies, but literacy itself, which audio does not: spelling,
punctuation, paragraphing and the ability to write are not things a listener
acquires.

## Drives

It drives sequential attention in a way nothing else here does. A medium that
will not let its reader skip produces readers who do not skip, and the habit of
holding a whole sentence rather than half-scanning it does not stay on the page.

It drives an exact relation to spelling, because the contracted grade wants both
how a word is spelled and how it is built, and a reader of it is often more
precisely aware of the shape of words than a person who has only ever seen them.

And it makes literacy independent of light and of line of sight. This can be read
in the dark, in a pocket, under a desk, without looking down, and by someone
whose eyes are doing something else entirely. Print asks for a lit page and a face
pointed at it. This asks for a hand.
