# Position: Diné bizaad

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

An Athabaskan grammar, whose nearest relatives are not neighbours: the family's other branches are spoken two and three thousand miles north, in Alaska and northwestern Canada, and the southern branch reached the Southwest comparatively late. The verb is the sentence. A single verb carries who acts, who is acted on, the mode, the aspect, and a classifier prefix that changes what the verb does with its participants, so one word does the work of an English clause and the parts cannot be lifted out separately.

It has classificatory stems, which are the thing anyone who has met this language remembers. There is no general verb for handing something over. The stem itself says what kind of object it is - a solid roundish thing, something slender and flexible, something slender and rigid, something flat and flexible, a mushy mass, a set of separate items, a load carried, an open container with contents - and there are about a dozen such sets. The shape and consistency of the thing handled sits inside the verb and cannot be left out.

It has a ranking of beings that constrains what may be said in what order: which participant is allowed to stand as the agent depends on where it falls in that ranking, so some sentences cannot be built the obvious way at all and have to be recast. It has high and low tone, nasal vowels and vowel length, all of them meaning-bearing. And it has a large aspect system in place of a simple tense one, so the same act is said differently according to whether it happened once, is under way, is repeated, or is finished.

## Orders

It orders the speaker to know what kind of thing they are talking about. You cannot hand someone an object, or put it down, or pick it up, without committing to its shape and consistency in the verb, and there is no vague form to retreat to. It orders the ranking: a speaker cannot make certain participants the agent of certain others, and must rebuild the sentence instead of forcing it. It orders aspect over tense, so the shape of the event is settled before the words leave. And it orders tone, because the same syllables at a different pitch are a different word, which is why the writing carries marks that a reader used to English keeps skipping.

## Loses

It is losing its children, and the reason is documented rather than mysterious. Children were punished for speaking it in government and mission boarding schools, and a generation that learned that lesson did not pass it on. It remains the most widely spoken Indigenous language north of Mexico, with something on the order of a hundred and seventy thousand speakers, and a minority of Navajo children now learn it first. A language can be the largest of its kind on a continent and still be going.

It loses on its own terms institutionally: the Navajo Nation requires fluency of its president, and the pool of candidates who can meet that test keeps narrowing, so a nation finds itself legislating for a language it is being separated from.

And there is an overlap it cannot make comfortable. In the same years that the United States was suppressing this language in its schools, it valued it as a code no enemy could read. Both things were happening, and to the same families. What is usually told as a single proud story - that Navajo speakers spoke Navajo and the other side could not follow - also flattens the work: the men who did it built a substitution code inside the language, with agreed words standing for letters and terms, because the language alone was not a cipher.

## Drives

It leaves its holder unable to be vague about objects, since a lifetime of choosing stems is a lifetime of noticing what kind of thing is in your hand. It trains a constant sense of rank among beings, because the grammar will not let that be ignored. It makes anyone who has held it and then learned English feel English as a language that lets you get away with saying almost nothing precisely.

And it asks something of its holders that no grammar accounts for: to carry a language that is held up as having helped win a war, and to remember at the same time who was punishing children for speaking it while that war was being fought. The language carries both facts, and there is no register in it that makes them fit together.
