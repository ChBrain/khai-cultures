# Position: British Sign Language

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

Simultaneity. A spoken tongue has one channel and must put everything in a
queue; this one has two hands, a face, a torso and the space in front of the
body, all running at once, so a single verb can carry who did it, to whom, along
what path, at what speed, with what attitude, in the time a spoken language needs
for the subject alone. Nothing about that is shorthand. It is a different
arrangement of the same grammar, and the information the queue would have spread
over a clause arrives in one movement.

A grammar on the face. Raised brows mark one kind of question and lowered brows
another; a headshake is the negation and not a comment on it; the mouth carries
adverbs, so the same sign for _drive_ is careful or reckless according to what
the lips are doing. These are not expressions in the sense a hearing person means
by the word. They are inflections, they are obligatory, and a fluent signer with a
still face is producing something ungrammatical rather than something flat.

And the space. Mention two people and you put them somewhere, one here and one
there, and from then on you point at them, move verbs between them and take
their positions when you speak as them. The result is a system of reference with
no ambiguity in it at all: there is no sentence in this language that means _he
told him he was wrong_ and leaves you wondering which one.

## Orders

It orders you to commit. You cannot introduce two people without placing them,
and once placed they stay placed, so the vagueness that a spoken pronoun allows
is simply not available. It orders the same about a room, a road or a
relationship: describe it and you have drawn it, at a scale and from a viewpoint
you have chosen, and having chosen you must keep to it.

It orders the eyes. This is a language that cannot be received by someone facing
away, taken in while doing something else, or half-heard from the next room. Both
people must be looking, for as long as it lasts, which makes an ordinary
conversation an act of attention that a spoken one does not require and cannot
enforce.

And it orders the face to work, which is the demand that most often defeats the
hearing learner. A person raised not to show what they feel has to unlearn that
before they can form a question, because in this language the composure is the
missing word.

## Loses

It cannot be written. Not badly, not partially, not yet: there is no orthography
this community uses to send a letter, keep a diary, sign a contract or publish a
novel. Notations exist and are read by researchers rather than by signers.
Everything this language has ever said survives as video, as a memory, or as a
translation into the written language of the country around it, so a tongue with
a literature, a poetics, a humour and eight hundred years of Deaf community
behind it has no archive in its own hand. This document is itself the proof: it
is a position about a language, written in another one, because there was no
alternative.

It loses the ordinary line of descent. Around nine in ten deaf children are born
to hearing parents, so most of its native users do not learn it from their
families. It passes sideways instead, from other children, in schools and clubs
and from deaf adults met later, which makes the school the family and gives the
language a transmission that any change of education policy can cut.

And it was cut. From the Milan congress of 1880 the schools across Europe turned
to oralism and the hands were sat on, sometimes literally, for the better part of
a century, on the argument that signing would stop a child speaking. It did not
stop the language, which went on in the playground and the club, but it produced
generations taught that their first language was a failure to have a proper one.
Britain recognised it in 2003 and legislated for it in 2022, which is recent
enough that people who were punished for using it are alive to see it.

## Drives

It trains the whole visual field. A signer takes in a face, two hands and a body
at once and reads a room the way a hearing person reads a tone of voice, and that
does not switch off when the conversation ends.

It drives directness, because the grammar will not help you be vague. A verb that
must show the path and the manner is a verb that has already said how hard you
hit it, and a language whose questions are on the eyebrows is one where the
question is visible before the sentence arrives. The reputation its community has
for bluntness is largely this: not a temperament, a grammar.

And it makes a linguistic community that nobody is born into by descent. Its
users mostly do not share ancestry, region, religion or family with one another;
what they share is the language and the places it is used, so belonging here is
constituted by the tongue itself rather than inherited alongside it. That is the
rarest thing in this package. Every other position in it is held by people who
were, in the main, handed it by their parents.
