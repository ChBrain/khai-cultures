# Position: English in braille

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

Six dots in two columns, sixty-three combinations and a blank, in a cell small
enough that a fingertip covers it whole. That last constraint is the design and
everything else follows from it: the cell is the size of the sensing organ, so a
reader takes in exactly one character at a time and never any part of the next.

Contractions, which are what make it usable rather than merely possible. In the
contracted grade a single cell can carry _and_, _for_, _the_, _with_, a two-cell
sequence can carry _knowledge_ or _mother_, and the commonest letter groups have
signs of their own. Without them the medium would be far too slow to read at
speed and far too bulky to shelve; with them a fluent reader keeps up with
speech, and pays for it by learning a second layer of spelling on top of English
spelling.

And a system in which reading and writing are the same physical act. The dots
that are read by the finger are punched by hand or by machine in the same
arrangement, so a person who can read this can write it, which is not something
print can say of handwriting.

## Orders

It orders one character at a time, forward. There is no glance, no peripheral
vision, no taking in the shape of a page before starting on it. A reader cannot
skim, cannot look ahead to see how long a sentence is, and cannot flick back to a
half-remembered line without hunting for it by hand. Print allows a reader to
cheat constantly and quietly; this does not allow it at all.

It therefore orders memory. Because the beginning of a sentence is behind the
finger rather than still on the page, a reader has to hold what has been read
rather than rely on being able to look again, and holds a great deal more of it
than a sighted reader of the same text does.

And it orders bulk. A cell cannot shrink below what a fingertip can resolve, so a
printed book becomes several volumes, and every practical question, what gets
transcribed, what a library stocks, what a child carries to school, is decided by
a physical constant that no technology has been able to argue with.

## Loses

It loses the glance, and with it the page. Layout, typography, a heading seen
before it is read, a table taken in as a shape, a footnote noticed at the bottom
without being sought: all of that is print doing two-dimensional work, and this
medium is strictly one-dimensional in time. What print carries in the arrangement
of the ink, this has to carry in words or lose.

It loses ground to speech. Audio arrives faster, costs nothing to produce and
needs no transcription, and the proportion of blind children taught to read this
has fallen a long way from where it was. What goes with it is not access to
books, which audio supplies, but literacy itself, which audio does not: spelling,
punctuation, paragraphing and the ability to write are things a listener does not
acquire.

And it loses whatever the language it carries has already lost. This is not a
tongue and it has no words of its own. Nothing in it differs from English in
grammar, vocabulary or sound, and a reader looking here for the kind of feature
that earns a variety a file elsewhere in this package will not find one, because
there is none to find.

## Drives

It drives sequential attention in a way nothing else in this package does. A
medium that will not let its reader skip produces readers who do not skip, and
the habit of holding a whole sentence rather than half-scanning it is carried out
of the reading and into everything else.

It drives a particular relation to spelling, since the contracted grade requires
knowing both how a word is spelled and how it is built, and a reader of it is
often more exactly aware of the shape of English words than a person who has only
ever seen them.

And it makes literacy independent of light and of line of sight. This can be read
in the dark, under a desk, in a pocket, without looking down, and by a person
whose eyes are doing something else entirely. Print asks for a lit page and a
face pointed at it; this asks for a hand.
