# Position: South Carolina English

## Taxonomy

Parent group: positions

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Has

South Carolina English is two Englishes split by the fall line. The Upcountry speaks the Inland South's, where a long i loses its glide and time comes out close to tahm. The Lowcountry spoke one of the strangest accents on the continent, and Charleston was its centre: the long a and long o of eight and boat held level or slid inward instead of upward, so eight came out close to ee-ut; the vowels of house and write rose before a voiceless consonant the way Canada's do; beer and bear were one word; and the r after a vowel was not spoken at all. That a long i kept its glide here, where the rest of the South let it go, was the surest way to hear that a speaker came from the old port and not from inland.

## Orders

Like all English it orders a fixed subject verb object sentence, an article before most nouns, and do before a question or a negative, because the verb's ending no longer says who acts. The Charleston register orders more on top of that: the vowel of a word decided before the mouth opens on it, and the r after a vowel left unsaid, so that the speaker is placed by the second syllable. It also orders a vocabulary the mainland never needed and took from the people who worked the rice: goober, gumbo, cooter for a turtle and juke for a roadhouse came into Carolina English through Gullah, which holds its own grammar in its own file and is not a register of this one.

## Loses

The Charleston vowels are going. Linguists who recorded the city across three generations found the ingliding eight and boat, the raised house and the merged beer and bear all retreating, the youngest speakers sounding like the rest of the Southeast, and the old sound surviving mostly in the oldest white and Black Charlestonians alike. With it goes the one audible boundary between Lowcountry and Upcountry. And because those words from Gullah passed into English without their grammar, the English that kept them is the reason the grammar gets heard as English spoken wrong.

## Drives

Raised in a register that marks where you come from on the first vowel, the Lowcountry speaker learns that an accent is an address, and that in this state an address was once a rank. The Upcountry speaker learns the same thing from the other side of the line. And anyone who says goober or cooter without knowing where the word came from carries, without choosing to, the fact that this English was shaped by people who were not allowed to write it down.
