## The defining question

**What defines Appenzell Ausserrhoden, and does the play stage it?**

The obvious answer is the one Innerrhoden owns: Landsgemeinde, Tracht, Alpstein,
the archaic democracy. Ausserrhoden shares the name and almost none of that, and
a play that staged it would be staging its neighbour.

What defines it instead is an absence, and the play is built on it. The canton
was created in 1597 as the part that was left over, with no capital and no centre
(`plot_00`); it made a virtue of that by turning every scattered farmhouse into a
workshop (`plot_01`); it lost the trade and a quarter of its people and never
recovered the population it had in 1910 (`plot_02`); and it gave away its
Landsgemeinde by its own hand in 1997, having granted women the vote by its own
hand in 1989, a year before the federal court forced Innerrhoden (`plot_03`).
`plot_99` holds the consequence: three regions that face outward in three
directions, and a de facto centre that lies in another canton.

The Arc, Name and Stakes all turn on the missing middle, and each of the five
plots stages it from a different side, so the words the play uses about itself
appear in its scenes rather than only in its description.

**What is deliberately not staged.** The Helvetic period and the 1798-1803
reorganisation are absent: they happened to this canton rather than through it,
and adding them would lengthen the chronology without answering the question.
The Pestalozzi children's village at Trogen (1946) is the strongest candidate for
a sixth plot - a canton that had emptied itself taking in other people's
children - and it is left out only because it belongs to Trogen more than to
Ausserrhoden. A later hand may disagree; that is the argument to answer.
