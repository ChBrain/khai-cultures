# Plot: The Navvies and the Nurses, 1945 to 1970

## Cue

A young woman answers an advertisement placed in an Irish provincial paper by an English hospital, and a young man from the next parish takes the boat to a building site, and between them they are the two commonest Irish lives of the decade, because at home there is no work and the state that won its freedom cannot yet feed the people who won it.

## Action

Through the 1950s the traffic runs one way and heavily: hundreds of thousands leave, the great majority for the far shore rather than for America, and the country they arrive in is short of hands for exactly the two jobs they take. They build the roads, the reservoirs and the power stations of the reconstruction, and they staff the new health service, which a Welshman founded and which recruits in Ireland openly and by name. They arrive to windows advertising rooms and specifying, in writing, who need not apply, and they take the rooms that will have them and send money home from them. Within a generation their children are English or Scottish and Irish at once, follow one country at football and another at rugby, and go back every summer to a place they are visitors in.

## Tension

Both countries tell this wrong and in complementary directions. Ireland tells it as a wound and rarely as a wage, though the remittances were holding up the west for twenty years. Britain tells it, when it tells it, as a story about prejudice, which it also was, and not as a story about dependence, which it more fundamentally was: the reconstruction and the health service were built in significant part by people the receiving country was simultaneously declining to rent rooms to. Neither version leaves room for the thing that actually happened, which is that two states became one labour market and then two families.
