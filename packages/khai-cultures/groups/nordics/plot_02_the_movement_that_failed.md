# Plot: The Movement That Failed and the Understanding That Did Not, 1840 to 1864

## Taxonomy

[play_nordics](play_nordics.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Students from Copenhagen, Lund and Christiania meet, and discover over several days of speeches that each of them can be understood by the others while speaking his own language, and none of them has studied the others', and they decide this must mean something.

## Action

They call it Scandinavism and they mean it politically: one north, standing together, three tongues that are obviously one family and a shared past to match. It is a movement of the reading public the church accidentally built, carried in newspapers and student societies and songs, and for twenty years it looks like the coming thing. Then Denmark goes to war over Schleswig in 1864 against Prussia and Austria, expecting the brothers, and the brothers do not come. Sweden-Norway stays out. Denmark loses a third of its territory and the political version of Scandinavism is finished in a season, and is never seriously revived.

What survives is the observation the students started from. The three languages go on being mutually followable, and a century later the north puts that in writing: a convention giving a citizen of any of these countries the right to use their own language in another's public offices and courts. The politics could not be built on the comprehension. The comprehension turned out not to need the politics.

## Stage

- [Denmark](@chbrain/khai-cultures-denmark/play_denmark.md): the country that went to the war expecting company and lost a third of itself without it.
- [Sweden](@chbrain/khai-cultures-sweden/play_sweden.md): the country that stayed out, and has been the north's cautious centre ever since.
- [Norway](@chbrain/khai-cultures-norway/play_norway.md): in union with Sweden at the time, and with a language question of its own that the movement could not help with.
- [det danske sprog](@chbrain/khai-cultures-tongues/da/position_language_da.md): the one the other two can read most easily and understand least easily, which is the joke the north has made about it since.

## Tension

A shared language is not a shared interest, and 1864 is where the north learned it in one go. What is left is stranger and more durable than what was lost: an arrangement in which three countries understand each other and owe each other nothing, and have built ninety years of practical cooperation on exactly that footing. Every Nordic institution since has been designed by people who remember that the family did not come, and has therefore been careful to ask for very little.
