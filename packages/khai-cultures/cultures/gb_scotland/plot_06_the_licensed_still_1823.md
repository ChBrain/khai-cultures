# Plot: The Licensed Still, 1823

## Cue

In the glens the still is hidden up the burn and worked at night, the duty is not paid and is not thought worth paying, the excisemen seize thousands of the things a year and the neighbours put up another, and the whole country agrees without discussing it that the smuggled spirit is the good one.

## Action

An Act cuts the duty and sets the licence at a price a working distiller can meet, and the trade comes down off the hill. The men who take the licence first are for a time in real danger from the men who do not, and one of them is said to have carried pistols for years. Within a generation the illicit still is a memory and the licensed one is an industry: the spirit is put in oak and left alone for years, which nobody had bothered to do when it had to be hidden, and it turns out that the waiting is what makes it. The glen's name goes on the cask, the cask goes to the world, and the poorest agriculture in Britain finds a way to send barley and peat abroad at a price nobody would have believed.

## Tension

The country's most famous product is made by leaving something alone for a decade, which suits a people who distrust display and admire a hard thing done well and quietly. But the dates do not sit comfortably beside each other. The glens acquire their world-famous names in the same years their townships are being emptied, so the label on the bottle is often the last public use of a place name whose people were put on a boat, and Scotland has never entirely decided whether that is heritage or an epitaph.
