# Plot: Hāloa, the elder sibling

## Taxonomy

[play_hawaii](play_hawaii.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Before any canoe and any chief, the genealogy already had the islands in it. Papahānaumoku and Wākea bear the islands themselves. Then Wākea and Hoʻohōkūkalani bear a child who does not live; he is buried, and from the grave the first kalo grows. The next child lives, and is named for him. Every Hawaiian descends from the younger. The elder is the plant.

## Action

This is not a founding. Nobody arrives, nobody is crowned, nothing is established. It is a statement about who is related to whom, and it is the statement the rest of this line keeps being measured against.

The order of birth is the whole of it. The islands are the eldest. Hāloanakalaukapalili, the stillborn brother who became kalo, is next. Hāloa the man, and everyone after him, is the youngest in the house. That ranking is not sentiment about nature; in a society that ordered everything by seniority, it is a legal position. The land outranks the people in it.

> The ʻāina is not where Hawaiians live. It is the older relative they live with.

From that come the obligations that run in both directions, which is what makes it kinship rather than stewardship. The elder feeds the younger: kalo is the staple, and the word for the family, ʻohana, is built from ʻohā, the shoot that comes off the corm. The younger tends the elder: the taro patch is kept, the water is kept running to it, and a thing that is not kept dies. Neither party is doing the other a favour, and neither can resign.

Everything downstream in this line is a question about that relationship being honoured or broken - who may divide the land, who may sell it, who may fence off the water, and whether a people who were never the senior party can be dispossessed of a relative.

## Tension

The strength of a people whose claim to a place is not conquest, purchase or charter, but descent - you cannot be a squatter on your own relative. And the exposure of exactly the same thing: a relationship is not a deed, and every legal order that has since reached these islands has asked for a deed. The question this line keeps returning to is whether a kinship can survive being asked to produce paperwork, and what is left of it when the paperwork is produced by somebody else.
