# Plot: the Māhele, 1848

## Taxonomy

[play_hawaii](play_hawaii.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Kamehameha III divides the land of the kingdom into three: crown lands, chiefs' lands, and lands the makaʻāinana may claim. The commoners are given two years to file. When the window closes they hold well under one percent of it.

## Action

The reform is written to protect the kingdom and it is the instrument that loses the land. Foreign powers were pressing, the argument for private title was that a country of registered owners is harder to annex than a country of customary tenure, and the king was advised by men who believed it.

The filing is what breaks it. A claim required knowing the process existed, reaching a land commission, describing a boundary in a form the commission accepted, and paying for a survey. The ahupuaʻa had worked for four centuries precisely because nobody needed a document; everyone knew where the lines ran and who was answerable. That knowledge was worth nothing at a counter.

> The system was not taken. It was converted into a form its holders could not fill in, and then the deadline passed.

What the makaʻāinana lost was not only acreage. An ahupuaʻa ran ridge to reef so that no household held only one part of a watershed; a fee-simple parcel has no such requirement. Land could now be sold, and within a generation it was, in blocks, to men growing sugar - the same interest that takes the government in 1893.

## Tension

A government reforming in good faith under foreign pressure, choosing the tool its advisers said would save it. And the plain result: a people who had held the land as the younger relative of it were asked to prove ownership of a family member, on a form, within two years - and the failure to file is now the legal record that they never owned it. Every land claim since argues with that record.
