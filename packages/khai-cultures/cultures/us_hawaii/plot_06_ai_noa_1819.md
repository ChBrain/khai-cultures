# Plot: the ʻAi Noa, 1819

## Taxonomy

[play_hawaii](play_hawaii.md)

## Owner

- Project: khai-cultures

## Cue

Six months after his father dies, Liholiho sits down and eats with the women of the court. Under the kapu that has governed the islands for centuries, men and women eat separately and the penalty for breaking it is death. Nothing happens. The temples are ordered burned and the images pulled down.

## Action

It is done from the inside, by the people the system most benefited, and it is done deliberately. Kaʻahumanu, Kamehameha's widow, and Keōpūolani, his highest-ranking wife, press the new king to it; the kahuna nui Hewahewa assents. The order goes out to destroy the heiau. The whole religious apparatus that tradition traces back to Pāʻao ends in a season, by the decision of those at the top of it.

The timing is the fact most often got wrong. **The missionaries had not arrived.** The first company of American Protestants reaches Kailua in the spring of 1820, months after the temples were already down. They did not break the kapu; they walked into the space where it had been.

> The Hawaiian state religion was not destroyed by conversion. It was abolished, and then something was brought in to replace it.

Why is arguable and argued: forty years of foreign ships had visibly not been punished for eating as they pleased; epidemics had killed on a scale the kapu had not prevented; and the ranking women had the most to gain from ending a system that ate separately. What is not arguable is who did it.

## Stage

- [Hawaiian culture](position_culture_hawaiian_culture.md) : the belonging surviving the removal of the religion that had ordered it.
- [hula storytelling](process_hula_storytelling.md) : the trained memory that carries what the temples held once the temples are gone.
- [Hawaiian sovereignty](position_hawaiian_sovereignty.md) : the kingdom exercising the deepest authority a state can, over the sacred, while still entirely its own.

## Tension

A people capable of dismantling their own oldest institution when they judge it has stopped working - the opposite of the frozen tradition outsiders came to describe. And what it cost: the year the temples came down is the year the islands had no established religion at all, and the ships arriving in the spring were carrying one. Whether that was a culture clearing its own ground or a culture opening its door at the worst possible moment is the argument this plot leaves standing.
