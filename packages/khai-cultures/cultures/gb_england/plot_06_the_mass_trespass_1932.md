## Cue

The high moor above the mill towns is grouse shooting kept private for a handful of guns a season, and the young men and women of the cotton city, who have a Sunday and no country to spend it in, decide on a spring morning to walk up onto it anyway.

## Action

Some four hundred ramblers set off from Hayfield on the twenty-fourth of April and go up onto Kinder Scout. Keepers meet them on the hill and there is scuffling; five are arrested, and five are sent to prison, one of them for six months, by a jury that includes two brigadier generals and several country gentlemen. The sentences do more for the cause than the walk did. Within two decades the argument is won on the page rather than the hill: an Act in 1949 opens access to the countryside and creates national parks, and in 1951 the Peak District, the moor they were jailed for walking on, becomes the first of them.

## Tension

The green and pleasant land the English keep as their self-image was, for most of them and for most of its history, somebody else's property, and the right to stand in it had to be taken by people willing to be jailed for it. The country now remembers the trespass fondly, which is a way of forgetting that it was a crime, and the pastoral picture is quietest about the fact that the picture is the prize in a fight.
