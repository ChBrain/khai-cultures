# Play: the Diné

## Estate

High desert across the corner where Arizona, New Mexico and Utah meet, twenty-seven thousand square miles of it, larger than ten states and fixed at its corners by four mountains. Coal under the north, uranium under much of the rest, a river along the boundary, and a tap in two houses out of three.

## Name

Diné, the people. Navajo is what the neighbours called them and what the treaties wrote, from a Tewa word for planted fields, and the nation carries it in its own legal title while using the other one at home. Both names are in daily use by the same people in the same afternoon, and neither is a grievance.

## Arc

They came from the north, stopped between four mountains and took up everything that was offered without becoming anything else: farming, sheep, horses, silver. Then they were starved out of a canyon, walked three hundred miles, and walked back, which no other removed nation did. What they have lost since has been taken by paperwork rather than by cavalry: the herds by a quota, the language by a school, the water by a ruling.

## Stakes

Whether what gets handed down is the language or only the membership. This nation is in no danger of ending, which makes it unusual in this house and changes the question: it has courts, a college, a press and a roll, and it has a fluency curve going the wrong way in every decade anybody has measured. A people can be entirely secure and still be losing the thing that made it itself.
