# Plot: The Twelve Miles of Water, from 500

## Taxonomy

## Owner

## Cue

People standing on the Antrim coast can see Scotland, twelve miles off on a clear day, while behind them the way south into the rest of the island runs through a belt of drumlins and bog that armies have always found harder than the crossing.

## Action

So the traffic goes east and west across the water rather than north and south down the island, and it goes both ways for a thousand years before anybody plants anything. A single kingdom, Dál Riata, holds ground on both shores in the sixth century and does not think of itself as divided by the sea; Gaelic goes over from here into Argyll and becomes the Gaelic of Scotland; galloglass come back the other way as fighting men for Ulster lords. Ulster ends up the most separate of the island's provinces, the last to be brought under control from Dublin or London, and the one whose nearest neighbour is not the rest of Ireland. When the Crown looks for settlers in 1609 it does not have to look far, and it does not send strangers: it sends people whose ancestors had been crossing that water in the other direction since before there was a border to cross.

## Stage

## Tension

The founding fact of this place is not a plantation and not a border; it is a geography that made Ulster face east. Everything afterwards is an argument about what that means. One tradition reads the crossing as an old and honest belonging, older than the Crown that formalised it; the other reads the same crossing as the route the dispossession came by. Both are looking at the same twelve miles of water, and neither reading is available without the other.
