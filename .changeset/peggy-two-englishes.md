Saxony's Peggy stops holding one English and starts holding the two she actually
has.

The link fix in the pass before this one was correct and incomplete. Moving her
from `en_us` to the `en` anchor stopped her claiming an American English nothing
in her file supported, but it left her with "English" as a single undifferentiated
thing, which is not what a German engineer in a Dresden fab has.

She has two, and they came from different places. The British one she was taught
at school, `colour` with a u and `in hospital` without the article, the spelling
whose consistency the classroom insisted on, and which she now only recognises
when she reads it. The American one is the industry's, carried by the tool
vendors, the specifications and the handbooks, and she wears it. The seam is
where the two files already disagree on the page: she writes `color` in the wafer
log and `colour` in a letter and no longer knows which is hers, and `gotten`,
which `en_gb` records as a distinction it lost, she hears every day and was never
taught.

This stages something no German culture in the house was staging - German school
English is British and German ambient English is American, and a German
professional lives in the gap - and it stages it where it belongs, on a persona,
because it is a relationship to a tongue and not a variety with features of its
own. No new tongue file.

Her old gap went with it. The baroque and Günter's porcelain reaching her as a
faint echo was never a language gap and was wearing `process_hearing_followed`;
it moves to her Shadow, where what her focus blinds her to already lives.

`REFERENCES.md` records why the occupation-zone account of German English does
not survive contact with Peggy - Saxony was the Soviet zone, where Russian came
first and English second on British norms - and leaves Günter for the next hand:
seventy-one, schooled in the DDR, and his file says he speaks no foreign
language. Staging the Russian he was made to learn and did not keep needs `ru`
moved into the tongues package first, the way `da` was.
