**Alsatian joins the tongues**, as a variety under the Alemannic anchor rather
than beside it. `gsw` is Alsatian's own ISO code and not a neighbour's: Alsatian
is Alemannic the way Swiss German is, so it hangs from
`gsw/position_language_gsw.md` and the tree needs no new shape for it. That is
the opposite call from Gallo in the same release, and for the opposite reason -
Gallo had no code to hang from, Alsatian has the anchor's own.

Every chapter is written to say what the anchor does not. What it gives: a second
lexicon three centuries deep, whose French stems take Alemannic endings and are
no longer heard as loans (`ich schenier mich`, `er het sich blessiert`); a
boundary inside itself, High Alemannic in the Sundgau, Low Alemannic in the
north, Rhine Franconian beyond that; and Orthal, a 2003 spelling most of its
speakers have never seen. What its grammar makes a speaker mark: which side of
the schooling they came up on, because every object newer than 1870 has both a
French and a German word and neither is neutral - a choice no other Alemannic
variety has to make. What it cannot say: itself in writing. What it drives: the
knowledge that shame works faster than prohibition.

The Loses chapter is the reason this file is worth having next to the anchor.
The anchor already says Alemannic loses itself at state borders, a dialect to be
ashamed of in one country and the educated colloquial in another, and that this
changes the standing and not the sound. Alsace is not another instance of that.
Every other Alemannic variety has a written standard above it from its own
family; Alsatian's was German, and German was taken out of the schools in 1945
as the occupier's language. It is the only Alemannic variety whose roof belongs
to another family - the difference is not regard, it is that Switzerland kept
its roof and Alsace's was removed.
