**Scotland's plot line stopped in 1750, and six of its twenty Company elements
were staged nowhere.**

The play ran Bannockburn, the Reformation, Culloden, the Enlightenment: four
plots, all of them before the Union had finished settling, and nothing at all
after the middle of the eighteenth century. Burns, Eilidh, the Saltire, whisky,
the ceilidh and Hogmanay were all in the Company and in none of it. Five plots
are added; the four that existed are unchanged except that plot 4 now carries a
date.

**`plot_00` Beyond the Wall, 122 to 563.** Rome builds a wall, pushes a second
one a hundred miles north, holds it about twenty years and comes back down. The
origin is the absence: no roads to speak of, no towns, no Latin administration,
and no inherited habit of being governed from far away. What arrives instead
comes by sea from the west, Gaelic out of Ireland into Argyll and Columba onto
Iona in 563, so the faith and the letters come over the Atlantic rather than up
the road. One kingdom is then assembled out of at least four peoples, which is
why this culture has three tongues in its cast and no single one to call its own.

**`plot_05` The Kilmarnock Edition, 1786.** A tenant farmer in debt with a berth
booked to Jamaica prints six hundred and twelve copies of his verse in the tongue
the drawing rooms apologise for. They sell out in weeks and the ship sails
without him. The Tension is the seam the country has lived on since: its
philosophers made their names writing an English better than the English, its
poet made his refusing to, and both are claimed with equal pride.

**`plot_06` The Licensed Still, 1823.** The duty is cut, the stills come down off
the hill, and the spirit gets left in oak for years because for the first time it
does not have to be hidden. The waiting turns out to be the craft. The dates do
not sit comfortably: the glens acquire their world-famous names in the same
decades their townships are emptied, so the label is often the last public use of
a place name whose people were put on a boat.

**`plot_07` The Schoolroom and the Mòd, 1872 to 1892.** The parish school, the
instrument of the levelling learning the country boasts about, is the instrument
that takes Gaelic out. The Act makes no provision for it, the playground punishes
it, and a generation of parents draw the obvious conclusion and speak English to
their own children, which does more damage than the punishment. Twenty years
later the same country founds a society in Oban and holds a festival with
adjudicators and medals. This is the hardest fact in the play and the one the
national self-image has least room for.

**`plot_99` The Question Asked and Left Open, since 1999.** The Parliament comes
back after two hundred and ninety-two years; September 2014 answers the question
fifty-five to forty-five on a turnout of nearly eighty-five per cent, the highest
Scotland has ever recorded; 2016 votes sixty-two to thirty-eight to remain, in
every one of thirty-two council areas, and the union it stayed in leaves anyway.
The Saltire flies over both campaigns, which is what makes it the nation's rather
than a party's. A record and not a forecast.

Every Cue was written against the passport test. The subjects are the peoples
watching an empire give up, a farmer with a berth booked, the men working a still
up the burn, a child punished in a playground, and a door opened at the bells. Not
one is the state.
