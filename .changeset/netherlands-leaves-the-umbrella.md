## plot_00: De dijk van de buurman

The Revolt is already `plot_01` and would be the passport besides. The origin is
what the ground demanded **centuries before there was a state**.

A few farmers stand on a terp on the northern coast, watching the water. It rises,
it rises every year, and nobody is holding it back but themselves. The man on the
next mound is no friend, **but his stretch of dyke keeps both their land dry.**

From about the year 1000 the people of this coast build dykes, and no one can do
that alone: **a dyke is as strong as its weakest stretch, and that stretch belongs
to the neighbour.** So the water boards appear - levying tax, electing officers,
issuing fines - and they exist before there are counts, provinces or a kingdom.
Whoever will not agree drowns with the rest.

**So the consultation is not a virtue here and not a character trait. It is a
technical requirement of the ground.** That is where `het poldermodel` comes from,
and it is worth saying plainly, because every later account of this country treats
its equality as temperament.

The Tension: it is an equality **born of necessity rather than generosity, and it
reaches exactly as far as the water does.** Whoever lives inside the dyke counts;
what lies outside is another matter. Which is also why an older language inside the
same dykes can quietly disappear with nobody putting anything in its way - and
`it Frysk`, the tongue of the people who were already on those mounds, is staged
there.

## plot_99: De borrel in het Engels

Friday, five o'clock, the office borrel. One colleague speaks no Dutch, and the
whole circle switches to English. Nobody asks, nobody minds, and **Riet notices that
in English she says slightly less than she would have said in Dutch.**

The country that was always proud of speaking everyone's language finds that its
own has become optional. Universities teach whole degrees in English and bring the
world in, until politics pushes back and moves to restrict it. **Daan** does his
study, his work and half his social life in English and finds that entirely normal.
At the other end of the same country **Baukje** passes on `it Frysk` to whoever will
still listen, at a kitchen table where the children speak Dutch to each other and
English on their phones.

**Two languages shift in the same direction at the two ends of one country, for the
same reason: nobody forbids anything, it is only easier.**

The Tension names the contrast with #621 outright, because the two look alike and
are not. **In Belgium two communities avoid each other's language and meet in a
third; here one community accommodates everyone so willingly that its own tongue
becomes optional at home. The mechanism is exactly reversed and the outcome
rhymes.** What the decade asks is not whether Dutch disappears, because it does not,
but whether the **equality** of the borrel survives being conducted in a language
nobody at the table learned first - because speaking as equals is not the same as
speaking equally well, and **the least fluent person stays quiet, even in the
flattest country on earth.**
