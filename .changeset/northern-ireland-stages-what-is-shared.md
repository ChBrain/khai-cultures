**Northern Ireland had four plots and all four were the constitutional story;
what both communities actually do all week was in the Company and staged
nowhere.**

The play ran the Plantation, Partition, the shipyards and the Agreement. Davy,
Niamh, the Ulster fry, the two tongues, Gaelic games and the Twelfth were all in
the Company and in none of it, which on this culture is not a small omission: the
Arc says two traditions holding one ground with equal dignity, and the traditions
themselves were the part that was missing. Five plots are added; the four that
existed are unchanged except that plot 3 now carries its year.

This is the culture where the passport test is hardest, because here almost
everything can be told as constitutional. Every new Cue was written against it and
the subjects are people standing on a coast, weavers at a crossroads, men taking a
room in a hotel, a boxer refusing a flag, and a gate that still shuts at night.

**`plot_00` The Twelve Miles of Water, from 500.** Ulster can see Scotland and
cannot easily reach Dublin, so the traffic runs east and west across the water for
a thousand years before anything is planted, both ways. Dál Riata holds ground on
both shores; Gaelic goes over and becomes the Gaelic of Scotland; galloglass come
back. When the Crown looks for settlers in 1609 it does not send strangers. The
founding fact of the place is a geography that made Ulster face east, and both
heritage tongues arrive by that crossing, which is why one of them shares its
negative with the Gaelic of Argyll and the other is a Scots that came back.

**`plot_05` The Diamond and the Lodge, 1795.** Linen puts weavers of both
religions into the same townlands competing for the same work; two armed factions
meet at a crossroads; the Protestant side founds a society of lodges the same day
and fixes a parade to the twelfth. What the Order does is turn scattered factions
into an institution with a rulebook and a calendar, and what the calendar does is
give a community one day a year on which it is unmistakably itself in public.

**`plot_06` The Parish and the Pitch, 1884 to 2001.** The GAA ties a club to a
parish and a parish to a county, nobody paid and nobody transferred, and in the
northern counties it becomes the parish's own ground for a community that has few
grounds of its own. It was also, for most of a century, a body of one tradition
with a rule barring the British forces, and the clubs voted that away in 2001 and
opened Croke Park four years later.

The two plots are written to be read against each other, and both end on the same
unsettled question: whether a body that is genuinely a culture and genuinely a
marker of one side can ever be read by the other side as the first thing rather
than the second.

**`plot_07` The Dove on the Shorts, 1985.** A featherweight from Clones, a Catholic
married to a Protestant, is told to pick a flag and declines both, comes in under
the UN dove with his father singing _Danny Boy_ where the anthem would be, and
takes the world title. The Falls and the Shankill cheer the same man on the same
night in the worst decade the place has had. The finding is exact and modest: the
appetite for a shared thing was always there and was never the difficulty; the
difficulty was that nothing in public life was arranged so both could hold it at
once, and a man had to invent a flag to get round it.

**`plot_99` The Fry and the Gate, since 2007.** The two hardest men sit down and
are photographed laughing; the institutions go down in 2017 and 2022 and come back
in 2020 and 2024; the 2021 census returns more people of Catholic than Protestant
background for the first time and three answers to the identity question with a
majority for none; around a hundred barriers still stand; fewer than one child in
ten is in an integrated school; and the same fry is on the same pans on both sides
of the wall. A record and not a forecast.

`position_culture_northern_irish_culture.md` gains the conformance links, and here
Britishness is unlike the other three: a live question rather than a background
fact, held by one tradition, declined by the other, and settled for both by an
agreement that put the right to be either, or both, in writing.
