**Sweden's Arc is about unwritten rules, and none of them were staged.**

Consensus over conflict, `lagom` as an ideal, the `folkhem` that keeps everyone in
and nobody too far out. The plot line said 1523, 1928, 1944 and 1921 — a
coronation, a speech, a rescue and a franchise. **Nine of eighteen Company
elements were the Arc's own material and staged nowhere**, the largest count of
the five: fika, midsommar, lagom, allemansrätten, the Dala horse, crispbread,
Gamla Uppsala, and both contemporary personas.

Four plots are added; the four that existed are untouched. Sweden's numbering was
already non-chronological, with 1921 sitting after 1944, so the appended block
follows the existing convention rather than forcing a renumber.

**`plot_00` Gamla Uppsala och tinget, från 500.** Three great mounds raised in the
sixth century by people who carried the earth by hand, on a plain with no harbour
and no defensive line — the place everyone came to anyway. The thing's business is
what shapes everything after: **a king is not king by inheritance. He is acclaimed
at the thing, and must then ride the Eriksgata and be received by each province
separately** before he counts. Power comes from below and in the plural, and a king
who needs twelve receptions cannot command, only negotiate.

One thing is deliberately not staged as record. Adam of Bremen's temple with its
nine-yearly sacrifice is a text written in the 1070s by a man who never went, about
a people his church wanted to convert. The mounds are certain; the temple is a
telling, and the plot says so.

**`plot_05` Kaffeförbuden, 1756 till 1823.** The crown bans coffee, and when that
fails bans it again, half a dozen times, seizing pots and fining drinkers — and
loses every time. The country ends up among the heaviest coffee drinkers on earth.
Nobody demonstrated and nobody wrote a pamphlet; people simply went on boiling
coffee in their kitchens until the state gave up. **That is the Swedish form of
disobedience, quiet and patient and without a scene, and it won.** What survives is
not the drink but the form: a pause everyone takes at once, sitting, with something
plain, where nobody performs and the absent are noticed.

Gustav III's twins are staged as the anecdote they almost certainly are, and the
plot says why it has lasted two hundred years.

**`plot_06` Semesterlagen 1938.** Two statutory weeks with pay meet a customary
right of access older than the statutes. Nothing granted allemansrätten; 1938 is
the year it suddenly applies to millions of people with time to use it. The same
year brings the Saltsjöbaden agreement, so the decade's two most Swedish events are
a holiday and a handshake. Midsommar takes its place, fixed to a Friday from 1953,
celebrated outdoors in a climate that does not guarantee it.

**`plot_07` Dalahästen i New York, 1939.** A carved winter pastime from one
province stands outside the pavilion at the World's Fair, three years after an
American book has named the country the middle way. Sweden discovers it has a
reputation, that the reputation is an asset, and that it consists largely of being
reasonable. It exports ships, steel and bearings, and what sticks is a horse
without a saddle and a word about moderation.

**`plot_99` Det kontantlösa landet, sedan 2015.** The country stops using money you
can touch, faster than anywhere, and again without a decision — the same movement
as the coffee, running the other way. Then the part that was not in the
description: `Astrid`, thrifty by habit, finds fewer places her way of paying
works, and the authorities begin asking people to keep cash at home. The country
joins an alliance in 2024 after two centuries outside, with the broad agreement it
is used to calling self-evident and which two years earlier did not exist. Fika does
not move, and is the only thing in the plot that looks the same as it did in 1938.

Every Cue has a non-state subject: people carrying earth to a mound, a drink the
crown could not stop, a worker with two weeks and no land, a photograph outside a
pavilion, and a sign in a café.
