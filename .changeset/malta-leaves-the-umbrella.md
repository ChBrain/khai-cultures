## plot_00: Qabel kulħadd

`plot_01` is St Paul around the year 60, which the play already treats as the
deepest root there is. The origin is three and a half thousand years older.

Around 3600 BC, on a dry rock in the middle of the sea, people cut soft yellow stone
out of the ground with tools of stone and antler and raise walls higher than a man,
built in a curve. **No metal. No empire behind them. No large tree and no river on
the island** - only the sea around them and the stone beneath.

These are the megalithic temples - Ġgantija on Gozo, Ħaġar Qim, Mnajdra, Tarxien -
**older than Stonehenge and older than the pyramids**, among the oldest free-standing
stone buildings anywhere in the world. Built from `il-ġebla tal-franka`, soft enough
to cut with a stone blade and hardening in the air. There was nothing else to build
with: **whoever lives on this rock builds with the rock.**

Then, around 2500 BC, **the temple people vanish.** No siege is recorded and no war.
The temples stop being built, the island goes quiet, and when people come again they
are other people and they bring another language.

Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, Byzantines, Arabs, Normans and the rest pass
through, and each leaves something. The tongue that stayed in the mouth is not the
first people's: it is a Semitic language that arrived after 870 and is written today
in Latin letters. **The one thing that never changed is the stone itself** - temples,
churches, the bastions of Valletta, the houses going up this year, all one material,
and the yellow face under the sun has been there for six thousand years.

The Tension: an island with nothing but stone learns one thing before all others -
build with what is under your feet, because there is nothing else. That is where the
fortress's strength comes from, and the craft of making something whole out of
little. **But it is also where the shadow comes from. The first people who built on
this rock vanished leaving neither a language nor a name, and the tongue the island
now guards as its own arrived on somebody else's boats. On a rock everybody wants,
belonging is not measured by who came first but by who stayed and what they held -
and that question reopens with everyone who steps off onto the quay.**

## plot_99: It-tmienja ta' Settembru

On 8 September `Mark` stands on the bastions above the Grand Harbour watching
`ir-regatta`. Below him the boats carry the colours of the harbour towns, as they
always have. **Around him on the bastion, half the people watching are not from the
island, and he cannot place all the languages he hears.**

The eighth of September is Jum il-Vitorja: the island remembers the end of the Great
Siege of 1565 and the day in 1943 that ended the wartime siege. **Two sieges, one
date.** The harbour towns row on the same water that once held the armies back.

But what changed the island in this decade did not come in under arms. The population
passed half a million and grew faster than any other country's in the Union, and it
grew almost entirely through people born elsewhere who came for work. Malta is the
most densely populated country in the Union. The cranes are everywhere: apartment
blocks faced with **a thin skin of `il-ġebla` over concrete**, fields going, and the
face of the harbour changed more in ten years than in the hundred before.

`Il-festa` holds. The parish, the band clubs, the fireworks and the statue in the
street are all still there, and `Ċettina` still takes her grandchildren so they know
who they are. But the village around the parish is not the same village: the flat
next door is let to people who leave within the year, and the feast is now also a
date in a visitor's calendar. At Marsaxlokk `il-luzzu` still carries the Eye painted
on its prow, but there are fewer fishermen every year and the boat is **photographed
more than it is worked**. The Eye looks out at a sea that today brings tourists,
workers, and boats of people fleeing - not armies.

The Tension: the island always measured itself by what it kept out, and on the eighth
of September it celebrates keeping out twice. **But what filled the island this decade
never knocked: it came in on a job and a lease.** `Żgħar imma sħaħ` was always measured
by faith, fortress and tongue - and today the feast is more intact than the belief,
the fortress has become construction, and Maltese is sinking under English in the
mouths of the people raised in it. The decade does not say whether an island that knew
itself by who it kept out can know itself by who it let in. **It says only that this
time nobody paid for it with a siege: the door opened, the island filled, and the
island grew rich.**
