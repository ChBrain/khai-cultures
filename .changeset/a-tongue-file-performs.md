**The references table was claiming a performance three files do not give.** The build's own
doctrine is that _"a tongue file is that tongue performed rather than described"_, but the
`Written in` column printed the orthography alone — so `nv` read **"the Navajo alphabet, with
tone and nasal marks"** while the file is written in English, and `xpu` read "the Phoenician
abjad" while being English too. Each file's `language:` front matter said `en` and the table
never surfaced it.

It now does, and it counts:

> **3 of 155 describe rather than perform** — `na`, `nv`, `xpu` — and each is a debt rather
> than a settled choice. English here is the last resort, taken only where nobody could write
> the tongue rather than where nobody could read it.

`gallo` is correctly **not** in that list: `fr-x-gallo` is Gallo performed, and the private-use
subtag is how BCP-47 says so. The test reads the tag by subtag rather than by prefix, which is
what tells those two cases apart.

The two provenance notes are reframed from settled choice to debt, and the reasons differ:

- **Nauruan** — the failure is the house's. The language is living, with speakers, a dictionary
  and a Bible translation, so somebody can perform this file and it should be rewritten by
  them. What would have been published otherwise is text no Nauruan speaker would recognise as
  Nauruan, under their language's name.
- **Punic** — the last resort is reached for a reason in the record. There is no continuous
  Punic prose to perform: the corpus is formulaic, plus the Poenulus passage. Four chapters of
  grammatical metadiscourse would be inventing a register Punic never had, in an abjad where no
  form could be checked and no reader survives to catch it. A rewrite needs an epigrapher
  willing to compose, which is a larger ask than a native speaker reading their own language.
