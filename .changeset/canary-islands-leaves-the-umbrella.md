The Canary Islands get a plot line and leave the umbrella. Fifth of the eight.

**Two plots.**

- `plot_00_agua_del_aire.md` — on the windward slope, halfway up, there is a
  forest sitting inside a cloud that does not move. It is not raining, and the
  ground is soaked. The leaves are combing the fog: each one collects what the
  air carries and lets it fall. On islands without a single river, that is the
  spring. These islands were never attached to any continent — they came up off
  the Atlantic floor one at a time, so everything living here arrived by air or
  by water and then managed alone, including a laurel forest that vanished from
  the rest of Europe millions of years ago. And one problem rules all the others:
  no rivers, little rain, porous ground. The only constant is the north-east
  wind, so the whole technique of this culture is getting water out of air. The
  forest does it by itself; where there is no forest, people do it — boring
  kilometres sideways into the mountain, and spreading _picón_, loose volcanic
  ash, because it lets dew down and will not let it back up. _Cultivar aquí no es
  sembrar en la tierra: es fabricarle a la tierra la condición que no tiene._
  And the other inheritance is stranger than it looks: people reached these
  islands from the African coast millennia ago and then there stopped being
  boats. By the time sails appeared again on the horizon, nobody here sailed —
  each island had become a separate world. On one of them, cut by ravines that
  take half a day to cross, they solved it by whistling, because a whistle
  crosses a ravine in a second and a man does not.
- `plot_99_lo_que_llega_por_el_mar.md` — the water account inverted in one
  generation. The galleries yield less, the cloud gives less, and the islands
  moved to desalination until the driest of them depend on it almost entirely.
  It works, at a price: the water no longer comes from the hillside, it comes
  from a motor, and the dry years of this decade brought declared water
  emergencies on the larger islands. Everything else now arrives by sea and air
  — some fifteen million visitors a year where a little over two million live,
  a disproportion that has not produced shared prosperity, since the archipelago
  holds some of the highest tourism figures and some of the worst income and
  poverty figures in the country at once. In April 2024 every island went out at
  the same time to say there is a limit, housing first. And people arrive who are
  not on holiday: the Atlantic route has had its highest numbers in these years
  and remains the deadliest of all, so the islands that lost their boats six
  hundred years ago are now the door the sea leaves people at. Meanwhile the
  ground did its own work — in 2021 a volcano erupted for nearly three months on
  one of the islands, swallowed whole neighbourhoods and thousands of banana
  plots, and left new hectares where there had been water.

The two ends meet on the same unfinished business. This culture became expert at
producing what the land would not give, and has ended up producing the water too.
The one thing it has never been able to manufacture is the decision about what
comes in by sea — and now everything comes in that way.
