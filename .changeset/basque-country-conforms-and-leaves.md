## plot_00: La lengua que vino de ningún sitio

`plot_01` is the abolition of the fueros in 1876, which is a grievance and not an
origin. The origin is a name and a grammar.

In a mountain valley a man is asked what he is called and answers with the name of the
[caserío](place_baserri_farm.md). It is not his father's surname: it is the name of the
house, which was there before his family and will be there after. **Whoever comes to
live in it takes its name. In this country you are not called after your father. You
are called after a building.**

And he says it in a language that resembles nothing else on earth. **Euskera is an
isolate**: not Indo-European, not Semitic, descended from nothing known, with no living
relatives anywhere. Every other tongue on the peninsula arrived - Celtic, Latin, Gothic,
Arabic, and the Romances that came out of Latin - **and this one was already here before
all of them, and still is.**

What it keeps is unlike them too: it marks differently the subject that acts on
something, it chains agreement with who acts, what is acted on and who it is done to
inside a single verb, and it counts in twenties, so seventy-five is three twenties and
fifteen. And the culture tells of itself that the words for cutting tools were built on
the root for stone, so that the axe carries the word for rock inside it - **an etymology
linguists dispute, though what telling it means is not in dispute**, which is the
consciousness of coming from long before.

From the house comes the rest. The baserri stands alone, governs itself, and is
inherited whole so it does not split. A country made of scattered self-administering
houses does not build the same ladder of lords as its neighbours, and its law is local,
customary and agreed, house by house and valley by valley. **From that come the
[fueros](position_foral_right.md), and from that comes `hitza hitz`, the given word -
the only contract possible between neighbours who have nobody above them to enforce
one.**

The Tension: a people whose language resembles nothing knows itself by difference and
not by kinship. **It cannot point to a cousin.** That is an extraordinary anchor and an
extraordinary isolation, because everyone around shares a family it is not in - hence
`hitza hitz` and the cuadrilla, since **when you cannot be understood from outside, the
group that does understand you is everything.**

And the shadow is the same virtue seen at night. **An identity founded on resembling
nobody has no natural way to include, and the tightest circle is also the hardest to
enter.** A culture that survived by closing has great difficulty opening afterwards, and
the twentieth century will show what that costs when the logic moves from the house to
politics.

## plot_99: Los que lo aprendieron

In the txoko, `Amaia` hears the youngsters at the next table speaking euskera - quick,
fluent, never hunting for a word. She understands every syllable and notices it is not
quite hers: no farmhouse intonation, none of the valley's words. **It is the standard,
the one from school. Not one of them learned it from anyone of her generation.**

This decade the Basque Country does the opposite of nearly every small language in
Europe: **euskera grows, and grows most among the young.** About a third of the
population speaks it, and among the under-25s the share is roughly double that of the
over-65s. **The oldest tongue on the peninsula is, in its own country, the language of
the people with the fewest years.**

It did not happen by itself. It happened through the ikastola, the school model, and
`euskara batua`, the unified form agreed in 1968 so that a language split across valleys
could have textbooks, news bulletins and exams. **The normalisation plan is a plan that
worked.**

And it worked with the bill written inside the result. **It came back through the
classroom and not the kitchen.** The young are fluent in batua, and the dialects Amaia
speaks in Gipuzkoa and `Iñaki` in the Bizkaian valleys are precisely the part thinning.
Knowing it and living in it are two different figures, and the distance between them is
the decade's real argument.

Around it the country changed shape. **ETA announced its dissolution in 2018, so for the
first time in sixty years there is a decade with no violence inside it.** The blast
furnaces of the Nervión that made Iñaki's pride are gone and Bilbao lives off museums
and services on the same estuary. What has not changed stays free: **the frontón is
still the one public building every village has**, and pelota is played there without a
ticket.

The Tension: **the Basque Country did what Galicia could not. It got its language back.
And it did it by exactly the method Galicia used to save its bagpipe - by teaching it.**
The rule turns up twice running in this walk and here it worked at full scale: **what is
taught survives, and what is assumed is lost.**

The bill is written inside the success. The tongue that came from nowhere and passed for
millennia from one valley to the next now passes in a classroom, in a unified form no
grandmother spoke, **and what is thinning is precisely what carried it this far. A
language saved is not the same object as a language kept**, and this culture now has
both at once and has to decide what to do with the difference.

And `plot_00`'s question is left standing. An identity built on resembling nobody works
while there is something to not resemble. **With no violence, no blast furnaces and the
language winning, the decade does not decide what it is to be Basque when there is no
longer anything to be Basque against. It only makes that, at last, the question.**
