## plot_00: Le Gibraltar du Nord

At the market at the foot of the rock, a trader looks up: a new flag is going up
over the fortress. She does not yet know which one. **The price of eggs does not
change, and neither does the language she haggles in.**

In 963 a count buys a small castle on a promontory, Lucilinburhuc, and the rock
turns out to be one of the most defensible points between France and the Empire.
**That is a misfortune dressed as luck: what is valuable to everyone belongs
lastingly to nobody.** Burgundians, Spaniards, French, Austrians and Prussians
fortify it in turn until it is the second strongest place in Europe and has earned
its nickname; it is besieged some twenty times. The people do not choose the
garrison. They learn to deal in the language of whoever holds the rock, to keep
their heads down, and to hold on to what cannot be occupied: the speech of the house
and the market.

**This is where `vivre en plusieurs langues` actually comes from** - not a policy but
a habit taken under successive garrisons.

The Tension: a rock everyone covets makes a people nobody consults, and everything
else follows from that - the flexibility, the multilingualism, the art of being
useful without standing out. But **an identity made of what the occupiers could not
take is an identity defined by others**: _remaining what we are_ does not say what
we are, only that we were not carried off. The question of what is inside that "we"
is deferred to a time when nobody besieges the rock any more.

## plot_99: Qui est d'ici

Late August, at the Schueberfouer, Lucien, Joaquim and Sandrine stand in the same
crowd at the same stall. **Tonight, two of the three will sleep in the country.**

An economy larger than its population is not run by its population. Nearly one
resident in two does not hold the nationality, and nearly half of those who work
here cross a border every morning and leave again every evening. **Joaquim** climbs
the steps one at a time, works in French and learns Luxembourgish in order to be
from here. **Sandrine** keeps the financial centre running in French and English and
goes home to sleep on the other side. **Lucien** switches language by counter
without thinking, and wonders now and then where the people who spoke like him have
gone.

The Tension: _mir wëlle bleiwe wat mir sinn_ was written against occupiers, and there
are none left. The sentence still holds, but **the question it asks has changed
sense: it is no longer who will take the country, but who is of it.**

And there is a detail this play carries without having meant to. **It stages French,
German and Portuguese as positions, and not Luxembourgish.** That is the language
which needs no position while everyone here is born with it - and it is the one
Joaquim learns in the evenings, because it has become the door. I have left that
observation in the Tension rather than inventing an `lb` position to tidy it: the
absence is the finding.
